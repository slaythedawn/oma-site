How to Become a Model With No Experience (What Agencies Actually Look For)

By Online Model Academy · The people who book models · August 2026 · 9 min read

Here is something that rarely gets said out loud in this industry: every working model you have ever seen started with zero experience. No portfolio. No walk. No idea what a casting even looked like from the inside.

At Online Model Academy, our instructors are the casting directors and agents who sit on the other side of the table. We review new faces for a living, and when an agency opens a submission from someone with no experience, nobody flinches. It is the default.

So if you have been typing "how to become a model with no experience" into a search bar and feeling like you are already behind, take a breath. You are exactly where every model starts. The real question is not how to hide your lack of experience. It is what to show instead. That is what this guide covers.

Why "No Experience" Is Completely Normal in Modeling

Modeling is one of the few industries where experience can work against you at the start.

Agencies sign potential, not resumes. A new face with no habits to unlearn, no dated photos circulating, and no history with other agencies is often easier to develop and place than someone who has spent two years doing the wrong kind of work. Scouts spend their days looking for people who have never modeled. That is literally the job description.

Think about what an agency does: it takes someone raw, develops them, builds their book through test shoots, and introduces them to clients. Development boards, new faces divisions, open calls: all of it exists specifically for people with nothing on their resume.

What trips up beginners is not the lack of experience itself. It is not knowing what agencies evaluate in its place. Get that right and "no experience" becomes what it actually is: a blank slate.

What Agencies Evaluate Instead of a Resume

When a casting director or agent looks at a new face, they run through a short mental checklist, and "experience" is not on it. Here is what is.

1. Your look, shown honestly

Agencies want to see you, not a produced version of you. That is why the industry standard for new faces is digitals (also called polaroids): simple, unretouched photos in plain clothing against a plain wall in natural light. No professional photographer, no makeup or very minimal, hair pulled back or worn naturally.

A standard set of digitals includes:

  • A front-facing headshot, relaxed expression
  • A smiling headshot
  • A left and right profile
  • A full-length shot, front and side
  • Clean, fitted basics: jeans and a plain tank or tee work fine

Digitals matter more than any portfolio at this stage because they are the one format agents trust. A heavily edited photoshoot tells an agent what a photographer can do. Digitals tell them what you actually look like walking into a client's studio on a Tuesday morning.

2. Your proportions and measurements, stated accurately

Know your real height and measurements and never fudge them. Agents measure new signings on day one, and a discrepancy costs you credibility before you have any. Commercial, fitness, parts, and curve boards are far more flexible than high fashion, so the point is not to fit one narrow mold. It is to know your numbers and submit to boards where those numbers make sense.

3. Your walk and how you carry yourself

For fashion and runway work, your walk is evaluated from the moment you enter the room. Posture, pace, how you stand while waiting, how you hold your shoulders when you think nobody is looking. Casting directors read all of it.

The good news: a walk is a trainable skill, and one of the fastest things a beginner can visibly improve. You do not need a runway to practice, just a hallway, honest feedback, and repetition. This is exactly why we made our runway masterclass module free at Online Model Academy. It is taught by the people who judge walks at real castings, and you can work through it before you ever attend one.

4. Presence and coachability

This is the one beginners underestimate most. In a casting room, agents are asking themselves a simple question: can I put this person in front of a client? That means eye contact, listening to direction, taking an adjustment without getting flustered, and being pleasant to spend ten minutes with. Clients rebook models who are easy to work with, so agents screen for it from your very first meeting.

You cannot fake presence, but you can build it. It comes from knowing what to expect, which is precisely what people with no experience lack and exactly what training fixes.

5. Reliability signals

Showing up on time to an open call, following submission instructions exactly, sending the photos that were asked for and nothing else. These sound trivial. They are not. Following directions precisely is the cheapest way for a beginner to look professional.

Your First Realistic Steps This Week

Not next year. This week. Here is a plan that costs almost nothing.

Day 1 to 2: Shoot your digitals. Find a plain wall with good window light and have a friend shoot them on a recent phone. Fitted basics, minimal makeup, no filters, no editing. Take more than you need and pick the clearest set.

Day 3: Take your measurements. Height, bust or chest, waist, hips, shoe size. Record them accurately.

Day 4: Build your target list. Look up licensed, reputable agencies in your region and find their "get scouted" or "apply" page. Nearly every legitimate agency has one, and it is free. Note each agency's submission requirements, because they vary.

Day 5: Submit. Send exactly what each agency asks for: a short, polite note, your stats, your digitals, your city. No life story. Agents open thousands of these. Clarity wins. If you are still a teenager, a parent or guardian has to be part of this step, and our guide to what the process looks like at 16 and 17 covers the extra permissions involved.

Day 6 to 7: Train your fundamentals. Practice your walk and your posing in front of a mirror or camera. This is also the ideal moment to go through the free runway masterclass, so your first casting is not the first time you have thought about any of this.

Then repeat. A non-response usually means "not what our board needs right now," not "you are not a model."

Building Early Experience Without Paying Scammers

Between "no experience" and "signed" is a middle zone where you can build real material. It is also where scammers concentrate, so let us handle both at once.

Test shoots (TFP). TFP stands for "time for prints" or "trade for photos." A newer photographer needs portfolio material, you need photos, nobody pays anybody. Look for local photographers whose existing work matches the genre you want to shoot, vet their full portfolio, ask for references, and bring a friend to the shoot if you want to. Two or three good tests can start a real book. When you are ready to get strategic about what goes in it, read our guide to building a modeling portfolio.

Local and small-scale work. Boutiques, hair salons, makeup artists building their books, community fashion shows, lookbooks for small labels. None of it is glamorous. All of it is reps: real cameras, real direction, real call times. Agents do not expect this work on your application, but the comfort it builds shows up in how you carry yourself.

Your own content. A clean, consistent Instagram or portfolio feed will not replace an agency, but it works as a living lookbook. Shoot simple, well-lit photos that show your face honestly and some range of expression, and skip the heavy filters. Agents who like your submission will look you up, and what they find should match your digitals.

Now the scam filter. Memorize this rule: money flows toward the model, never away from the model. Legitimate agencies earn commission when you book work. They do not charge you to join or condition representation on buying their photographer or "portfolio package." Red flags that should end a conversation immediately:

  • Any upfront fee to be represented or "considered"
  • A required photoshoot with their in-house photographer at your expense
  • Street or DM scouting that turns into a sales pitch for a paid convention or showcase
  • Pressure to decide on the spot
  • Guarantees of work (no honest agent guarantees bookings)

Paying for your own training or your own independent test shoots can be a legitimate investment you choose to make. Paying an "agency" for the privilege of being on their roster never is.

When Does Experience Actually Start to Matter?

Later than you think, and in a different form than you think.

For getting scouted or signed to a development board, experience matters close to zero. Agencies expect to build you.

Experience starts to matter once you are competing for direct bookings. Clients casting a paid campaign want evidence you can perform on set: hold direction, work through outfit changes, deliver usable frames efficiently. That evidence is your book and your reputation, both built through the test shoots and small jobs described above. In other words, experience is something the industry helps you accumulate after you are in the door. It is not the price of entry.

What actually gates your progress early on is skill: your walk, your posing, your understanding of how castings work, your on-set etiquette. Unlike your height, every one of those is learnable, and learning them before your first casting is the biggest edge a beginner can give themselves. FAQ: Becoming a Model With No Experience

Can I really get signed with zero experience?

Yes. New faces divisions exist specifically for undeveloped talent, and scouts actively look for people who have never modeled. What you need is not experience but accurate digitals, honest measurements, and submissions to agencies whose boards fit your look.

Do I need professional photos to apply to agencies?

No, and sending them can work against you. Agencies ask for unretouched digitals: phone photos in plain clothing against a plain background. They want to see exactly what you look like, not what a retoucher can do.

How old do you have to be to start modeling?

There is no single answer. Fashion agencies often scout in the mid-teens with parental involvement, while commercial, fitness, and lifestyle modeling regularly book models in their twenties, thirties, and beyond. Submit to divisions that match your age and look rather than assuming you missed a window.

How much does it cost to become a model?

Starting out should cost almost nothing: phone digitals are free and agency submissions are free. A legitimate agency never charges you for representation. Your only optional early costs are training you choose and independent test shoots you arrange yourself.

How do I know if a modeling agency is legitimate?

Legitimate agencies make money from commission on work you book, publish their signed models publicly, and never require upfront fees or purchases from their in-house vendors. If representation depends on you paying for something first, walk away.

How long does it take to start booking work?

It varies widely with your market, your look, and how much you develop your skills. Some new faces test within weeks of signing and book small jobs within months, while others develop for a year before their first significant booking. What you control is the speed of your skill development: your walk, your posing, and your casting readiness.
