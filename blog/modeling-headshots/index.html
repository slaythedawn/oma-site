Modeling Headshots: What Actually Gets You Booked

By Online Model Academy · The people who book models · September 2026 · 9 min read

Search "modeling headshot tips" and almost everything you find was written by a portrait photographer trying to book more headshot clients. That is useful information about lighting and lenses. It is not the same as knowing what a booking agent actually does with the image thirty seconds after you submit it.

Here is that version instead: what a modeling headshot is actually for, what a booker is really checking when they look at it, and why the answer changes depending on which kind of headshot you are shooting.

What a Modeling Headshot Actually Is

A modeling headshot is a clean, shoulders-up image of your face, shot against a plain background with even, flattering light. That sounds close to an actor's headshot, and the two do overlap at the edges, particularly for commercial and theatrical crossover castings. But the job they do is different.

An actor's headshot has to communicate a type: the roles a casting director could plausibly see you playing. A modeling headshot has a narrower job. It has to show your actual face, clearly and honestly, so a client or agent can judge bone structure, symmetry and skin the same way they would in person. Personality matters less here than accuracy. A headshot that flatters you into looking like someone else has failed at its one job, even if it is a beautiful photograph.

What It's Actually For

A headshot rarely stands alone. It shows up inside three different tools, and which one changes what the image needs to do:

  • On your comp card. The single image on the front is almost always a headshot, chosen specifically because it is the one thing a client will remember you by after a casting day full of faces.
  • Inside your portfolio. A strong clean headshot is the opening image in almost every working book, because it is the first thing a reviewer judges before deciding whether to keep scrolling.
  • In an agency submission. Alongside your digitals, a headshot gives a scout the clearest single read on your face, separate from the unposed proof-of-look the digitals are doing.

Notice what is missing from that list: a headshot is not itself a digital. A digital is deliberately unretouched and unposed, meant to show what you look like on an ordinary day. A headshot is still meant to be accurate, but it is a considered, chosen image rather than a raw proof shot. Confusing the two is one of the more common beginner mistakes.

What a Booker Is Actually Looking At

Strip away the photography advice and a booker's checklist is short:

  • Even, honest lighting. Flat and diffused, not dramatic. Hard shadows and heavy contouring lighting hide the exact structure a booker is trying to see.
  • A plain, uncluttered background. A wall, nothing behind you competing for attention. Nobody is judging your set design.
  • Minimal or no makeup. Enough to look rested, not enough to change your face. A booker who cannot picture your bare skin cannot judge whether you fit the job.
  • Direct eye contact. It reads as confident and gives the viewer something to land on immediately.
  • A retouch level you could stand next to. If a client meets you in person and you look meaningfully different from your headshot, that gap costs trust, not just this booking but the next one too.

Everything else, the lens, the studio, the exact software used to edit, is a photographer's concern, not yours. A phone shot with clean light and no filter beats a technically superior photo that hides who you actually are.

Commercial Headshot vs. Fashion Headshot

This is the split that trips up more beginners than any lighting or wardrobe question, because the correct answer to "how should I look" is genuinely different depending on the register you are shooting for.

Commercial (the smiling headshot)

The client is selling approachability: a real, warm, eyes-first smile, relaxed shoulders, an expression that would work on a billboard or a product page. This is the headshot that gets used for lifestyle, catalog and everyday-brand work.

Fashion or straight (the beauty shot)

Neutral or softly intense, not smiling. The point is to let an agent see your bone structure, jawline and proportions with nothing performative in the way. This is the headshot most fashion and editorial boards lead with, and it is the one new faces underestimate, because a neutral expression can feel like doing nothing when it is actually doing the most technical work in the shot.

A working portfolio usually carries both, not one or the other. If you only have time or budget for one image, ask what kind of work you are actually chasing before you choose.

What to Wear

Simpler than most guides make it sound. Plain, fitted, solid-color basics: a crew neck or scoop neck top in a neutral tone that does not fight with your skin. Skip busy patterns, visible logos, statement jewelry and high collars, all of which pull the eye away from your face when your face is the entire point of the image. If you are shooting both a commercial and a fashion headshot in the same session, a simple change of top is enough, you do not need a wardrobe change.

DIY, a Friend With a Phone, or a Professional

Not every stage of your career needs a professional photographer for this.

Before you are signed: agencies evaluate new applicants on clean, unretouched digitals, and a strong clean headshot at this stage can genuinely be a friend with a decent phone camera, daylight and a plain wall. Paying a photographer before an agency has looked at you is money spent before you know what your agency will actually want.

Once you are signed: a professional headshot becomes worth the cost, usually with a photographer your agency already works with and trusts, since consistency with their existing roster matters more than any individual photographer's personal style.

For freelance and direct-booking work: see our guide to freelance modeling, since without an agency vetting photographers for you, the responsibility for getting this right sits with you alone.

How Often to Reshoot

Whenever your look changes meaningfully, a significant haircut, a colour change, a visible change in body composition, and otherwise on roughly the same cycle as the rest of your comp card and digitals. A headshot that no longer matches how you actually look is worse than an older but honest one, because the gap is what erodes trust at the casting, not the age of the photo itself.

Mistakes That Cost Castings

  1. Heavy retouching. If a client meets you and you look different from your headshot, you have burned trust before the conversation even starts.
  2. Busy backgrounds and props. Nothing should compete with your face for attention.
  3. One expression doing every job. A single smiling shot cannot cover both a commercial casting and a fashion one. Shoot both if you can.
  4. An outdated image. Hair, colour or body changes that your headshot has not caught up with.
  5. Filters. Even a subtle one shifts skin tone enough that a booker notices, and it reads as something to hide.

Where This Fits

A headshot is one piece of a small set of tools that each do a specific job: your headshot for the first impression, your comp card as the physical leave-behind, your full portfolio to show range, and a written modeling resume for the freelance and crossover bookings that ask for one instead. Get the headshot right and the rest of the book has something honest to build around.
