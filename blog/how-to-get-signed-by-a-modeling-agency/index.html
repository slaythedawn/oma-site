How to Get Signed by a Modeling Agency: What Agents Actually Look For

By Online Model Academy · The people who book models · August 2026 · 10 min read

Most advice about how to get signed by a modeling agency is written by people who have never sat on the agency side of the desk. They tell you to be confident, drink water, and believe in yourself. Nice, and useless.

This guide is different. At Online Model Academy, our courses are taught by working casting directors and agents, the people who open the submissions and decide who gets signed. Here is what happens inside the agency when your email lands, and how to make yours the one that gets answered.

What Agencies Are Actually Looking For When They Scout

An agency is not looking for the most beautiful person in the room. An agency is looking for someone they can sell.

That distinction changes everything. Agents earn commission, so every signing is a bet. When a booker looks at your photos, they are asking: which clients would book this face, at what rate, and how often. A striking look that fits no client brief is a harder sell than a less dramatic look that fits twenty briefs.

Practically, agencies are evaluating a few things:

  • Fit for a board. Agencies organize models into divisions, often called boards: fashion, commercial, curve, fitness, petite, parts, and so on. You are not applying to "the agency" in general. You are being assessed for a specific board, whether you know it or not. Knowing which board you realistically fit is half the battle, and our guide on how to choose a modeling agency covers it in depth.
  • Proportions and measurements, honestly reported. Different boards have different ranges, and those ranges are wider than they used to be, especially in commercial and curve. What has not changed is that agents spot inflated measurements instantly, usually at the first fitting. Lying on a submission does not get you signed. It gets you remembered for the wrong reason.
  • Skin, hair, and teeth in good condition. Not perfect. Good condition. Clients pay for retouching, but they do not want to pay for a lot of it.
  • Something distinctive. A gap in the teeth, unusual coloring, striking bone structure, a memorable presence on camera. "Pretty" is everywhere. "I cannot stop looking at this digital" is rare, and it is what makes an agent walk a card to a colleague's desk.
  • Signals that you will be easy to work with. A polite, complete, well-organized submission suggests a model who will show up on time and take direction. Agents notice, because flaky talent costs them money and client relationships.

Notice what is not on the list: expensive professional photos, modeling school certificates, or thousands of followers. A large following can help in some markets, but no legitimate agency requires it, and none require paid portfolios from new faces. Prior experience is not on the list either, and our guide to applying when you have never modeled explains what agents weigh in its place.

The Submission Process Done Right

Almost every agency accepts online submissions through a website form or a dedicated email address. This is where most aspiring models sabotage themselves, so here is what the agent wants to receive.

Digitals: the photos that actually matter

Digitals, sometimes called polaroids, are simple, unretouched photos that show exactly what you look like right now. For a new face, they matter far more than professional shots, because a phone photo taken in daylight cannot lie.

Shoot your digitals like this:

  • Natural daylight, near a window or in open shade
  • Plain background, fitted clothing: jeans and a simple tank or tee
  • Minimal or no makeup, hair off your face
  • No filters, no retouching, no sunglasses, no hats

The standard set: a front headshot, a profile headshot, a smiling shot showing your teeth, and full-length front, profile, and back shots, taken by a friend at chest height. If you have professional images, include two or three of your strongest, but never edit your digitals. When you are ready to build a fuller book, our guide on how to make a modeling portfolio covers what comes next.

Measurements: exact, current, honest

Include your height, bust or chest, waist, hips, shoe size, and hair and eye color, measured the same week you submit. Agents build client packages around these numbers, and a model who arrives two sizes different from her card creates a problem for everyone. If the agency's submission form asks for a written summary alongside your digitals, our modeling resume guide covers what a short, honest one should include.

What to write

Short. Bookers review large volumes of submissions and open yours looking for a reason to keep reading. Give them the facts fast:

  • Your name, age, and city
  • Your measurements
  • One or two lines: availability to travel, relevant experience if any, and which board you think you fit if you know
  • Your Instagram handle if your account is presentable, since many agents will glance at it

Do not write your life story or explain that modeling has been your dream since childhood. Every submission says that. A clean, factual email with great digitals stands out because it looks professional. And follow the agency's submission instructions exactly. If the site says "no attachments, links only," send links. Ignoring instructions is the fastest way to be deleted unread.

Open Calls vs. Online Submissions vs. Getting Scouted

There are three doors into an agency. Knock on more than one.

Online submissions are the default. Every legitimate agency reviews them, though response times vary from days to months. The advantage is scale: you can submit to every appropriate agency in your market in an afternoon. The disadvantage is volume, which is why your digitals carry so much weight.

Open calls are scheduled times when an agency sees walk-ins in person. Not every agency runs them anymore, so check the current policy on their website. When offered, they are worth attending: an agent sees your proportions, skin, walk, and personality in real time, and you often get a reaction on the spot. Dress simply, have your digitals ready, arrive early, and expect the whole interaction to last two minutes. Two minutes of genuine attention from a booker is worth more than most people realize. An open call runs on the same etiquette as any other casting, which our walkthrough of what happens inside the casting room covers minute by minute.

Scouting is when the agency finds you: at a mall, a concert, an airport, or increasingly through Instagram and TikTok. You cannot force scouting, but you can be findable. A public account with a few clean, unfiltered photos showing your face and full body, plus your city in the bio, does more for discoverability than a thousand edited selfies. One caution: real scouts never ask for money and will direct you to verify them through the agency's official channels. If someone who "discovered" you starts talking about fees, walk away.

What Happens After an Agency Shows Interest

This is the part almost no article covers, and where preparation pays off.

The meeting. If an agent likes your submission, you will be invited in, in person or over video. Expect them to take fresh digitals, measure you, ask you to walk, and ask about school, work, travel flexibility, and how soon you could start. This meeting runs in both directions. You are allowed, and expected, to ask: Which board would I be on? What is your commission? What do you see as my market? Agents respect a new face who asks smart questions.

The test shoot. Many agencies arrange a test shoot with a photographer they trust before or shortly after signing, so they have current images to develop your book and show clients. Practices vary: sometimes the agency covers it, sometimes the cost is advanced against future earnings, sometimes photographers shoot new faces on trade. What should not happen is a demand for a large upfront payment to the agency itself as a condition of being signed. That is not developing talent. That is selling photography, and it is the oldest scheme in this industry.

The contract. Legitimate offers come in writing, and no legitimate agency pressures you to sign on the spot. Take the contract home, read it, and have a lawyer or an experienced adult read it with you. Any agent worth signing with will respect that.

Red Flags in Modeling Contracts

From the agent's side of the desk, here is what should make you pause:

  • Upfront fees. Real agencies earn commission on the work they book you. If the business model is charging models, it is not an agency. It is a sales funnel wearing an agency's clothes.
  • Mandatory in-house classes or photographers at your expense. Training can be valuable, but a contract that requires you to buy the agency's own classes or shoots is a conflict of interest.
  • Vague or missing commission terms. The percentage the agency takes should be stated plainly, along with any other fees deducted from your pay.
  • Extremely long exclusive terms with no exit. Multi-year worldwide exclusivity with no obligations on the agency's side and no termination clause for you is a cage, not a career.
  • No accounting terms. The contract should say when and how you get paid after a client pays, and give you the right to see statements.
  • Pressure tactics. "This offer expires tonight" is a sales technique, not how talent development works.

A good contract is boring. It states the commission, term, territory, scope, and payment terms, and both sides know exactly what they agreed to.

Why Rejections Happen, and How to Resubmit

Now the part nobody enjoys: most submissions are passed on, including many from people who later sign elsewhere and work steadily.

Here is what rejection usually means from the inside: the board is full for your look right now. The agency signed someone similar last month. Their clients are not booking your category this season. Your digitals were too poor to evaluate. You submitted to a fashion board when you are a commercial fit. None of those mean you cannot model. Agents pass on faces all the time and sign them a year later when the board opens up.

Treat rejection as data, not verdict:

  1. Submit widely. Different agencies serve different clients. A pass at one office says nothing about the next.
  2. Check your category. If fashion boards keep passing, look honestly at commercial, curve, fitness, or your local market.
  3. Fix your digitals first. Bad photos are the most common and most fixable reason for a pass. Holding your set next to books that actually get models signed makes the gaps obvious fast.
  4. Wait, then resubmit. Roughly six months is a sensible interval, or sooner if something real changed: new measurements, a new market, dramatically better digitals, published work. Note in one line that you are resubmitting and what is new. Agents do not penalize polite persistence, and sometimes it works.

Give Agents a Reason to Say Yes

It all comes down to one principle: agencies sign people who make the agent's job easier. Clean digitals, honest measurements, a professional submission, realistic targeting, and calm persistence put you ahead of most of the pile before anyone even evaluates your look.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to get signed by a modeling agency?

There is no standard timeline. Some models sign from their first round of submissions, while others submit for a year or more before the right board has room. Speed depends on your market, category, and digitals more than on luck.

Do I need professional photos to get signed?

No. Agencies prefer simple, unretouched digitals for new faces, because they show exactly what you look like. Professional photos become important after signing, when your agency helps you build a book matched to their clients.

How much does it cost to sign with a modeling agency?

Nothing upfront. Legitimate agencies earn commission from work they book for you. Development costs like test shoots may later be advanced against earnings, but a demand for payment as a condition of signing is a red flag.

Can I get signed without modeling experience?

Yes. Agencies sign new faces with zero experience all the time, and many prefer it. What you need is the right look for one of their boards and a professional, honest submission.

What age do modeling agencies sign new models?

It varies by board and market. Fashion boards often develop talent in the mid to late teens with parental involvement, while commercial, curve, and lifestyle boards sign models across a wide range of adult ages. Check each agency's requirements before submitting.

Should I submit to multiple agencies at once?

Yes, unless you are already under an exclusive contract. Submitting widely is normal, and if more than one agency makes an offer, you are in the strongest position to compare terms and choose the best fit.
