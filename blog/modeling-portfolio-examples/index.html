Modeling Portfolio Examples: What a Book That Gets Signed Actually Looks Like

By Online Model Academy · The people who book models · August 2026 · 9 min read

Search for modeling portfolio examples and you will mostly find website templates. Pretty layouts, zero information about what should actually be in the portfolio. But the people who decide your career, agents, bookers, and casting directors, do not care about your website theme. They care about the images, their order, and what those images prove about you.

At Online Model Academy, our courses are taught by working casting directors and agents, the people who flip through hundreds of portfolios a week. This article is built from that side of the desk: what a strong portfolio contains in each major category, image by image, with the reasoning behind every slot. Use these described examples as a literal shot list.

How Reviewers Actually Look at Portfolios

A portfolio review is fast. The first image gets a real look. The second and third get a glance. By image four, the reviewer has usually formed an opinion and is scanning to confirm it. Your portfolio is not a gallery of your favorite photos. It is an argument, and the opening images are the thesis.

Reviewers look for specific proof points: Do you photograph consistently from different angles? Can you move? Do you understand your own category? Is there anything a client could book today? Every image below earns its slot by answering one of those questions. Each example also flags which shots reviewers skip, because what you leave out matters just as much.

Example 1: The New Face Portfolio (Digitals-Led, 6 to 8 Images)

This is the portfolio for someone with no professional tearsheets. Agencies expect that, and most new face scouting decisions are made from digitals: simple, unretouched photos in plain clothing against a plain wall.

Image 1: Full-length digital, front-facing. Fitted jeans and a plain fitted top, natural light, plain background. It opens the book because it answers the first question any agent has: what do you actually look like?

Image 2: Head-and-shoulders digital, no smile. Hair pulled back, minimal makeup, eyes straight into the lens. This shows bone structure and skin. Reviewers linger here longer than anywhere else in a new face book.

Image 3: Head-and-shoulders digital, smiling. Same setup, real smile with teeth, proving your face has range and showing your teeth for commercial clients.

Image 4: Profile digital. Chin level, either side. Profiles reveal jawline and nose line for beauty and editorial work. Skipping this reads as hiding something.

Image 5: Three-quarter turn, full length. Shows how your body reads at an angle, which is how most set photos are shot.

Image 6: One simple personality image. A candid-feeling shot where you look relaxed and alive. This is what makes a reviewer think there is a person here, not just measurements.

Optional 7 and 8: A swim or fitted-athletic digital if you are pursuing fashion, and one strong test shoot image if you have one.

What reviewers skip past: filtered selfies, prom or event photos, busy backgrounds, and group shots. If a reviewer has to figure out which person you are, the image is already dead.

Example 2: The Fashion and Editorial Portfolio (10 to 12 Images)

For models pursuing high fashion, runway, and magazine work. The standard here is mood, angles, and the ability to wear a concept, not just clothes.

Image 1: A strong beauty shot. Tight crop, striking makeup or completely clean, intense eye contact. Fashion books open on the face because the face is the product.

Image 2: Full-length editorial in strong styling. Elongated pose, deliberate hands. This slot proves you understand shape-making, the difference between standing in clothes and modeling them.

Image 3: Movement shot. Fabric in motion, a walking stride, a jump held cleanly. Show bookers want evidence you can move, and this image is the closest a page gets to a walk.

Image 4: Black and white portrait. Stripping color forces the viewer to see structure and emotion; a book without one reads as one-note.

Image 5: An unconventional angle or crop. Shot from below, extreme side light. Editorial clients hire models who look interesting in imperfect conditions.

Image 6: A second full-length, different silhouette. If image 2 was sleek, this one is voluminous or deconstructed. Range within the same category.

Image 7: Editorial story image. A frame that feels pulled from a magazine story, signaling you can hold a concept for a full shoot.

Image 8: Detail or beauty-adjacent shot. Hands, jewelry, a fragrance-style composition. Quiet images suggest versatility for beauty and accessories clients.

Images 9 to 12: Your strongest tests or tearsheets. Published work goes here if you have it. If not, close with your two best test images and one clean digital so the reviewer can calibrate everything against reality.

What reviewers skip past: five variations of the same pose. One shoot should never occupy more than two slots. Repetition reads as a thin portfolio wearing a costume.

Example 3: The Commercial Portfolio (8 to 10 Images)

Commercial modeling sells products, so this book is warmer, brighter, and more literal. The reviewer is often a client, and clients cast for relatability.

Image 1: The money smile. Bright, genuine, teeth showing, clean daylight. Commercial casting starts and ends with whether people would trust you.

Image 2: Lifestyle-in-action. Drinking coffee, walking a sidewalk, mid-laugh. It should look like a stock photo in the best sense: instantly usable.

Image 3: Business or smart-casual look. Blazer, clean grooming, confident but approachable. This slot books office-adjacent campaigns in tech, finance, and healthcare.

Image 4: Athleisure or active image. Not a fitness shot, just healthy and energetic. Sneaker brands, wellness apps, and pharmacy chains cast from this frame.

Image 5: Interaction-style image. Reacting to something off camera, as if mid-conversation. Commercial shoots run on believable reactions.

Image 6: Warm casual look. Softer styling, relaxed setting, widening the range of campaigns you fit.

Images 7 to 10: Range fillers with intent. A second smile in different styling, one non-smiling headshot, and your best remaining images. Close on a simple, honest frame.

What reviewers skip past: moody editorial images in a commercial book. They do not make you look versatile, they make the reviewer wonder if you know what you are selling.

Example 4: The Fitness Portfolio (8 to 10 Images)

Fitness books get judged on two things: conditioning and believable movement. In that order.

Image 1: Full-length physique shot in fitted athletic wear. Clean light, honest conditioning, strong but natural posture. This is the qualifying image; everything after it is elaboration.

Image 2: True movement image. Mid-stride, mid-lift, mid-jump, with real effort in the body. Fitness clients spot a faked exercise instantly; a genuine one earns instant credibility.

Image 3: Smiling athletic portrait. Energy and approachability. Most fitness campaigns are aspirational-friendly, not intimidating.

Image 4: Detail-of-form shot. Back, shoulders, or legs, depending on your strongest feature. Apparel clients cast specific body parts more often than people expect.

Image 5: Sport-specific image, only if you genuinely play. Castings will ask you to perform it.

Image 6: Athleisure lifestyle frame. Post-workout feel, warmth, daylight. This bridges you into commercial wellness work, where the steady booking lives.

Images 7 to 10: A second physique angle, one clean headshot, and your best remaining frames.

What reviewers skip past: gym mirror selfies and over-filtered physique shots. If your digitals do not match your portfolio's conditioning, the portfolio gets discarded, not the digitals.

Example 5: The Teen Portfolio (6 to 8 Images, With Parent Notes)

Teen portfolios should be the simplest books of all, and the best ones are.

Image 1: Clean, natural headshot with a real smile. Minimal makeup, natural hair. Agencies scouting teens are casting potential, not polish.

Image 2: Full-length in simple, age-appropriate clothing. Jeans and a tee. Proportions and posture, nothing more.

Image 3: Non-smiling headshot. The face at rest, hinting at editorial potential.

Image 4: Personality frame. Candid energy, true to the teen's actual character.

Images 5 to 8: A profile digital, a three-quarter shot, and at most two simple test images.

Notes for parents: Legitimate agencies do not require paid photo shoots; clean phone digitals taken at home are enough to get evaluated. Styling should always be age-appropriate, and anyone pressuring you otherwise is a red flag, not an opportunity. Keep every image recent, because teens change quickly. And a parent should be present at every shoot, full stop.

What reviewers skip past: adult-styled images on minors. These do not just get skipped, they raise concerns about the adults involved.

Common Portfolio Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Too many images. Cut to your best 8 to 12. Reviewers judge you by your weakest image, because it shows your judgment.

Every photo from the same shoot. Maximum two images per shoot. If that leaves you with four images, you need another test shoot.

No digitals. Add them, even in an experienced book. Digitals are the truth layer that lets a reviewer trust everything else.

Wrong category signals. Decide what you are selling before you shoot. A book that is half moody editorial and half catalog smiles reads as undecided, and undecided does not get booked.

Old images. If your hair, weight, or look has changed, reshoot your digitals this week. Showing up to a casting looking different from your book burns client trust fast.

Opening weak. Your single strongest, most category-defining image goes first. Never open with a warm-up.

How Many Images Is Right?

Fewer than you think. New faces: 6 to 8 digitals. Developing models: 8 to 10 images. Established books: 10 to 12, refreshed as stronger work replaces weaker. If you are debating whether an image is good enough, it is not. A short book of consistent quality signals a model who understands the business. FAQ

What should a beginner modeling portfolio look like? Six to eight clean digitals: a full-length front shot, smiling and non-smiling headshots, a profile, a three-quarter turn, and one natural personality image. Plain clothing, plain background, natural light, no heavy editing.

Do I need professional photos to start a modeling portfolio? No. Legitimate agencies evaluate new faces from phone-shot digitals and often prefer them because they are honest. Invest in test shoots once you understand your category.

How many photos should be in a modeling portfolio? Between 6 and 12: 6 to 8 digitals for new faces, 8 to 10 for developing models, 10 to 12 for established books. Quality beats volume.

Should my portfolio mix commercial and editorial photos? Not in the same lineup. If you work in both categories, keep separate galleries and send the one that matches the client.

How often should I update my modeling portfolio? Refresh digitals every few months, or immediately after any change to your hair, weight, or overall look. Retire anything that no longer looks like you.

What is the difference between digitals and a portfolio? Digitals are simple, unretouched snapshots showing how you look right now; a portfolio is your curated professional work. Agencies use digitals to verify and portfolios to imagine. A complete submission includes both.
