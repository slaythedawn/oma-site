How to Become a Model: The Honest Guide From People Who Book Models

By Online Model Academy · The people who book models · August 2026 · 11 min read

Most advice on how to become a model is written by people who have never sat on the other side of the casting table. This guide is different. At Online Model Academy, our instructors are working casting directors and agents, the people who decide who gets signed and who gets booked. Here is what they actually look for, what the process really costs, and how long it genuinely takes.

One thing to get out of the way first: you do not need to pay anyone to "become" a model. Legitimate agencies make money when you make money. If you remember nothing else from this article, remember that.

Do You Have What Agencies Are Looking For?

The first question every aspiring model asks is "am I tall enough?" or "am I pretty enough?" Those are the wrong questions. The right question is "which market fits what I already have?"

Agents do not sign people who almost fit a category. They sign people who clearly fit one. So before you spend a dollar or send a single email, figure out where you actually belong.

High Fashion and Runway

This is the most restrictive category and the one most people picture when they think of modeling. Typical ranges agencies work within:

  • Women: roughly 5'9" to 6'0", slim build, generally scouted between ages 14 and 22
  • Men: roughly 6'0" to 6'3", slim to athletic build, generally scouted between ages 16 and 25

These are not arbitrary numbers. Runway samples are cut to a single size, and designers need every model to fit the same clothes. That is the entire reason the requirements exist. Exceptions happen, but they are exceptions, and building a plan around being the exception is a bad plan. The men's side of the market runs on its own boards, rates, and timelines, which we break down in our guide for men entering the industry.

Commercial Modeling

Commercial modeling is where most working models in the US actually earn their living. This is catalog work, e-commerce, lifestyle campaigns, and advertising. The requirements are far more flexible:

  • Height matters less. Women from about 5'6" and men from about 5'11" are common, and plenty of booked commercial models fall outside even that.
  • Age barely matters. Brands need faces that look like their customers, which means twenties, thirties, forties, fifties, and beyond.
  • The look that books is "relatable and polished," not "unusual and striking."

If you have an expressive face, good skin, straight teeth or a great natural smile, and you photograph warmly, commercial is likely your lane, and it is a genuinely good lane.

Other Categories Worth Knowing

  • Curve and plus: one of the faster-growing boards at major agencies. Height standards still apply at the fashion level, usually 5'8" and up for women, but commercial curve work is more flexible.
  • Fitness: defined muscle tone and athletic ability. Brands often want models who can actually perform the movement on camera.
  • Petite: primarily commercial, beauty, and parts work, since runway height standards do not bend much.
  • Parts modeling: hands, feet, hair, legs. Niche, competitive, and real. Hand models book steady work for jewelry, tech, and beauty brands, and our guide to working as a hand model explains what that niche really screens for.
  • Fit modeling: you are the body brands use to test garment sizing. Unglamorous, consistent, well paid, and almost never discussed in beginner guides.

An honest self-assessment here saves you months. A 5'4" aspiring runway model is set up to fail. A 5'4" aspiring commercial and beauty model is set up to work.

How to Become a Model: The Step-by-Step Path

Here is the path that casting directors and agents on our teaching staff recommend, in the order that actually works.

Step 1: Shoot Your Digitals (Not a Portfolio)

This is the single most misunderstood step, and it is where scam operations make most of their money. You do not need a professional portfolio to get signed. You need digitals, sometimes called polaroids: simple, unretouched photos that show an agent exactly what you look like today.

The standard set:

  • A front-facing headshot, no makeup or truly minimal makeup, hair off your face
  • A full-length shot in fitted, simple clothing (a plain tank and jeans, or a simple swimsuit)
  • A profile shot
  • A three-quarter shot
  • One natural smile

Shoot them in daylight against a plain wall on a phone. That is not a budget compromise. That is the professional standard. Agents want to see raw material, because they know exactly what their clients can do with it. Heavy editing, filters, and glamour lighting hide the information they need, and experienced bookers read retouched submissions as a red flag, not a selling point. It is also why a complete beginner starts at no disadvantage here, something we cover in our guide to starting out with zero credits.

Step 2: Research Agencies That Match Your Category

Do not blast one email to fifty agencies. Make a target list of ten to fifteen agencies that actually have a board for your category. If an agency's website shows no commercial division, your commercial submission is going straight to the trash no matter how good you look.

For each agency, find the "Get Scouted," "Become a Model," or "Applications" page and note their exact submission requirements. Then follow those requirements to the letter. Agents tell us that a shocking share of submissions get deleted unread because the applicant ignored the instructions. Following directions is the first professional test, and most people fail it.

Step 3: Submit Online and Attend Open Calls

Most agencies now take submissions through a website form or a dedicated email address. Your submission should include your digitals, your accurate stats (height, measurements, shoe size, clothing size), your age, your city, and one or two lines about yourself. Keep it short. Agents review submissions in seconds, not minutes.

Open calls still exist and are still worth doing if you live near a major market like New York, Los Angeles, Miami, or Chicago. An open call is a scheduled window when an agency meets walk-ins in person. Wear simple fitted clothing, keep makeup minimal, bring printed digitals if requested, and be ready for a short conversation. Agents consistently say the same thing: they are evaluating your energy and professionalism as much as your look, because they are deciding whether they can put you in front of a client.

Step 4: Understand What Happens If an Agency Says Yes

If an agency is interested, expect a meeting, possibly a request for new digitals, and then a contract. Read it. Standard agency commission in the US is 20 percent of your earnings, and many contracts also allow a service fee charged to the client. What a legitimate contract will not include is an upfront fee to join. Take your time, ask questions, and if you are a minor, involve a parent or guardian at every step. A real agency expects this and respects it.

Step 5: The Freelance Route (If Agencies Are Not Biting Yet)

Not getting signed immediately does not mean the answer is never. It often means "not yet" or "not in this market." Meanwhile, you can work.

Freelance modeling means booking jobs directly without an agency: local brands, boutique e-commerce, hair salons, photographers building campaigns, and small catalog shoots. You find work through your own outreach, a clean Instagram presence that functions as a portfolio, and legitimate casting platforms. You negotiate your own rates and keep 100 percent of the fee.

The freelance route demands more business skill from you, and you must vet every client yourself, but it builds three things agencies love to see in a future submission: on-set experience, tear sheets, and proof that clients hire you. Plenty of signed models started as freelancers who built enough of a track record that an agency came to them.

What It Really Costs to Become a Model

Here is the breakdown nobody publishes, because the honest answer does not sell expensive packages.

What getting signed should cost you:

  • Digitals: free, shot on a phone
  • Agency submissions: free
  • Open calls: free, plus your travel
  • A legitimate agency contract: zero dollars upfront

What you will genuinely spend money on as you start working:

  • Basic wardrobe for castings: simple, fitted basics you likely already own
  • Skin, hair, and fitness upkeep: whatever your normal routine costs
  • Travel to castings and jobs, which is usually on you
  • Comp cards once you are working, often coordinated through your agency
  • Education, if you choose it, from instructors with verifiable industry careers

Notice what is not on the list: modeling schools that promise agency placement, paid "model searches," conventions with entry fees, and mandatory portfolio packages. Which brings us to the most important section in this guide.

Modeling Scams: How to Spot Them Before They Cost You

Aspiring models are one of the most heavily targeted groups for scams, and the schemes work because they exploit hope. Casting directors on our team see the aftermath constantly. Here are the red flags they tell every new model to memorize.

  • You are asked to pay to be represented. Real agencies earn commission on your bookings. Any "registration fee," "website fee," or "board fee" charged before you earn is a business built on charging models, not booking them.
  • You must use their photographer. A legitimate agency may suggest photographers once you are signed and working. A "sign today, but first buy our $1,500 portfolio package" pitch is the single most common scam structure in the industry.
  • You were scouted somewhere strange with huge urgency. Real scouts exist, and they hand you a card and invite you to verify them and follow up through official channels. Scammers pressure you to commit on the spot, often at malls, online, or through unsolicited DMs.
  • Guarantees of work. Nobody legitimate guarantees bookings. Clients make booking decisions, not agencies, and any promise otherwise is a lie.
  • Instagram "castings" from unverifiable accounts, especially anything that asks for money, gift cards, deposits, or personal financial information, or that promises payment by check before a job. Check-overpayment fraud specifically targets new freelance models.
  • Requests for photos that make you uncomfortable, at any age, under any justification. Professional casting never requires this. Leave, block, and if a minor is involved, report it.

The pattern underneath every scam is the same: money or leverage flows from you to them before any real work exists. Legitimate modeling flows the opposite direction.

How Long Does It Take to Become a Model?

Honest ranges, based on what agents and casting directors see across real careers:

  • Signing with an agency: anywhere from weeks to a year or more of submissions, depending on your market fit and location. Some people get signed off their first digitals. Most do not, and resubmitting to the same agency after six months with fresh digitals is normal and expected.
  • First paid booking after signing: commonly a few months, because agencies first develop you, test you, and introduce you to clients.
  • A consistent income: for most working models, one to three years of building client relationships, in both the agency and freelance routes.
  • Fashion careers: often front-loaded and short, which is why agents push young models to save aggressively.
  • Commercial careers: can genuinely run for decades, because the market always needs faces at every age.

If your plan requires quitting your job the month you start, you do not yet have a plan. Every credible person in this industry will tell you to keep your income while you build.

FAQ: How to Become a Model

How do I become a model with no experience?

Shoot simple digitals on your phone, research agencies with a board that fits your category, and submit through their official channels or attend open calls. Agencies expect zero experience from new faces. What they evaluate is your raw look, your stats, and your professionalism. Experience is what they help you build after signing.

How much does it cost to become a model?

Getting signed should cost you nothing beyond travel. Digitals are phone photos, submissions are free, and legitimate agencies never charge upfront fees. Your real early costs are casting travel, basic grooming, and any education you choose. Anyone charging you for representation itself is a red flag.

Can I become a model at 5'2" or 5'4"?

Not in high fashion runway, realistically, because sample sizing drives strict height standards. But commercial, beauty, parts, petite, and fit modeling all book shorter models regularly, and commercial work is where most US models earn most of their income. Pick the category that fits you and target agencies with that board.

How old do you have to be to become a model?

Fashion agencies typically scout between roughly 14 and 22 for women and 16 to 25 for men, with parental involvement required for minors. Commercial modeling has no meaningful age ceiling, because brands need faces that match their actual customers at every stage of life.

Do I need professional photos to apply to a modeling agency?

No, and sending professional retouched photos can actually hurt you. Agencies specifically ask for clean, unedited digitals in natural light so they can see exactly what you look like. Save professional shoots for after you are signed, when your agency guides the direction.

How do I know if a modeling agency is legit?

Check that it earns through commission, not upfront fees. Verify a real client list and a track record you can trace, look for models who actually work, and confirm any scout through the agency's official website rather than a DM. If money is requested before you earn anything, walk away.
