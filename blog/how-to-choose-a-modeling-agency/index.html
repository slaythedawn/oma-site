Agencies

How to Choose a Modeling Agency (What Insiders Look For)

By Online Model Academy · The people who book models · August 2026 · 10 min read

Here is something most aspiring models never hear: agencies are not doing you a favor by signing you. An agency is a business partner that takes a commission on everything you book, which means you are hiring them as much as they are choosing you. That mindset shift changes everything about how to choose a modeling agency. You stop asking "will anyone take me?" and start asking "who can get me in front of the clients I want?"

At Online Model Academy, our instructors sit on the other side of the table: casting directors and agents who see which agencies command respect in a casting office and which get ignored. This is how they would vet an agency as a new face today.

First, Understand What Kind of Agency You Are Looking At

Not all agencies do the same job, and signing with the wrong type is one of the most common early-career mistakes.

Boutique agencies vs. major agencies

A major agency has name recognition, offices in multiple markets, and direct lines to the biggest clients. The tradeoff: big agencies carry big boards, and if you are one of two hundred faces you can get lost while bookers push whoever is hot right now.

A boutique carries a smaller board, often a few dozen models. You get more attention, more development, and a booker who knows your name and your book. The tradeoff is reach and negotiating leverage.

Insider take: for a new model, a strong boutique that develops you well usually beats a major that shelves you. Great tearsheets open doors that a famous logo cannot, and you can always move up later.

Mother agencies

A mother agency discovers and develops new talent, then places you with partner agencies in other markets. They are your home base: they manage your overall career, coordinate with placement agencies, and take a cut of what those placements earn.

A good mother agent invests in your development before you earn anything and protects you when foreign agencies push bad terms. A bad one is just an extra commission on everything you make. The difference is verifiable: real mother agencies have documented placements you can check, and can tell you which markets they plan to send you to and why.

Commercial vs. fashion boards

Inside most agencies, models are grouped into boards, essentially divisions.

The fashion board (sometimes split into editorial, runway, and image) serves designers, magazines, and high-end campaigns. Measurement and height requirements are stricter, rates are volatile, and prestige is the currency.

The commercial board serves advertising: catalog, e-commerce, lifestyle campaigns, product work. Requirements are more flexible, the work is steadier, and day rates for consistent commercial models are often better than people assume. Depending on the market there are also curve, fitness, parts, and lifestyle boards.

None of these is a lesser path. Some of the busiest models in any city are commercial faces you have never heard of. The right question is not "which board is more glamorous?" but "which board matches my look, my measurements, and my market?" An honest agency tells you where you fit. A dishonest one tells you whatever gets you to sign.

How to Vet a Modeling Agency Like an Insider

Once you know what type of agency you are dealing with, put them through the screening a casting director would.

What legitimate agencies always do

  • They make money when you make money. Commission on booked work, typically around twenty percent from the model's side in the US, is the entire business model.
  • They scout and review applications for free.
  • They give you a straight answer about your fit, even when the answer is no.
  • They put terms in writing and give you time to review the contract.
  • They have a verifiable track record: real clients, real bookings, real models you can look up.

What legitimate agencies never do

  • They never charge a signing, registration, or "onboarding" fee to join the board.
  • They never require you to buy a photoshoot from their photographer or a "partner" studio as a condition of representation.
  • They never sell mandatory classes as the gateway to being signed.
  • They never pressure you to sign on the spot. Only scams need urgency.
  • They never make income promises, because no agent controls what clients decide.

One nuance: agencies sometimes advance legitimate costs like comp cards or test shoots and recoup them from future earnings, which is standard practice. An advance comes out of money you earn later, itemized. A scam demands your credit card today and picks the vendor for you.

Check the bookers, not just the brand

The agency name gets you in the door, but your booker runs your career day to day. Before signing, find out who would actually represent you and look into their history. Bookers move between agencies and their client relationships move with them; an experienced booker at a mid-size agency often has stronger pull than a junior booker at a famous one.

Check placements and the board itself

Spend an hour on the agency's website and social channels and read them like an insider:

  • Are the models on the board actually working? Look for recent tearsheets, campaign credits, and runway imagery, not just test shots. Pretty pictures with no published work means a development mill.
  • Do their models appear on client work you can verify? Campaigns and editorials are public. If an agency claims major brand relationships, some of their models should be visible in that work.
  • Do models like you exist on the board? If you are a curve model and the board has no curve division, they have no clients for you.
  • How long do models stay? Boards that fully turn over every year are churning through hopefuls, not building careers.

Also check licensing where your state requires it. California and New York, among others, regulate talent agencies, and the databases are public.

The Scam Patterns That Target New Models

Scammers target new models because the industry's rules are unwritten. These are the three patterns insiders see most often.

1. Upfront fees dressed up in official language

The oldest scheme in the book: you are "accepted," and acceptance costs money. The fee might be called registration, website placement, portfolio hosting, or a background check. The label changes; the direction of the money does not. If you are paying to be represented, you are not being represented. You are the customer.

2. Pay-to-shoot portfolio schemes

This one works because it contains a grain of truth: models do need photos. A "scout" tells you that you have huge potential, but first you need professional pictures, and conveniently they have a photographer. The shoot costs hundreds or thousands of dollars, the photos are mediocre, and the representation never materializes. The photographer and the "agency" split your money. That was the entire transaction.

The legitimate version: a real agency signs you first, then arranges test shoots with photographers they trust, at low cost or advanced against earnings. Representation is never conditional on buying a shoot.

3. Fake scouting DMs

Instagram and TikTok DMs from "scouts" at famous agencies are now the most common entry point for scams. The playbook is flattery, urgency, and a link: they compliment your look, claim a client needs someone like you this week, and push you toward a form that harvests personal information, a payment page, or a request for photos you would not want in a stranger's hands.

Real scouting through social media does happen, but real scouts direct you to the agency's official application channels, never a payment link. Verify every time: find the agency's site yourself and ask whether the person who messaged you works there. Real agencies answer happily, because impersonation damages them too.

Questions to Ask Before You Sign

Walk into any agency meeting with these questions. How they respond tells you as much as the answers.

  1. Who would be my booker, and how many models do they manage? You want a named human, not "the team."
  2. Which board would you place me on, and which clients do you see me working for? Vague answers mean no plan for you.
  3. What is your commission, and are there any other costs I should expect? Get every number in writing.
  4. How do you handle expenses like comp cards and test shoots? Advanced against earnings is normal; payable upfront to their vendor is a red flag.
  5. What does my first six months of development look like? Good agencies have an answer.
  6. Can I take the contract home and have it reviewed? Any resistance ends the meeting. Politely leave.

An agency that respects these questions will respect you as a professional. One that bristles at them was never going to.

Exclusivity and Contract Basics in Plain Language

Modeling contracts are shorter than most people fear, but a few clauses do the heavy lifting.

Exclusive vs. non-exclusive. Exclusive means the agency is your only representation, in one market (common and reasonable) or worldwide. Worldwide exclusivity is a serious commitment; do not grant it to an agency with one office and no placement history. Non-exclusive means you can hold representation with multiple agencies, common in commercial markets. Neither is bad in itself; the scope just has to match what the agency will actually do for you.

Term and renewal. One to three years is typical. Watch for automatic renewal clauses that quietly extend the deal unless you object in writing within a narrow window. Calendar that date the day you sign.

Commission. Confirm the percentage taken from your rate. In many markets agencies also charge the client a separate service fee, which is standard. You are checking that your side of the math matches what they told you.

Expenses. The contract should itemize what gets charged back to you, such as comp cards and website fees, recouped from earnings rather than billed upfront.

Termination. You want a clear exit: how either side ends the agreement, with how much notice, and what happens to confirmed bookings. A contract you cannot leave is a contract you should not enter.

If a contract confuses you, that is not a personal failing. Having a lawyer or an experienced mentor read it before you sign is money well spent, and legitimate agencies expect it.

Choosing Well Is Half the Career

The right agency multiplies everything you do. The wrong one wastes your best years or takes your money and gives nothing back. Vet the type, vet the bookers, vet the board, ask direct questions, and read every clause. That is the whole method, and almost nobody entering this industry does it.

FAQ

How do I know if a modeling agency is legitimate?

Legitimate agencies earn commission on work you book and never charge fees to join, require you to buy their photoshoots, or sell mandatory classes as a condition of signing. Verify by looking up their models' published work, researching the bookers, and checking state licensing where it applies.

Should I sign with a boutique or a major agency?

New models often develop faster at a strong boutique, where a smaller board means real attention from bookers. Majors offer bigger client access but can shelve new faces. Judge each agency by its development record and whether models like you are visibly working there.

Do I need a mother agency?

Not always, but a good one is valuable if you plan to work in multiple markets. A mother agency develops you and places you with partner agencies abroad, taking a share of those earnings. Ask for verifiable placements before signing; if they cannot name any, the title is just a word.

How much commission does a modeling agency take?

In the US, agencies commonly take around twenty percent from the model's side of a booking, and many also charge the client a separate service fee. Figures vary by market, so get the structure in writing.

Is it normal to pay for a portfolio before signing with an agency?

No. Paying a photographer chosen by an "agency" as a condition of representation is the classic pay-to-shoot scam. Real agencies sign you first, then arrange test shoots at low cost or advanced against earnings. Representation should never be sold to you.

What should I do if a scout DMs me on Instagram?

Do not click links or send money or personal information. Find the agency's official website yourself and ask whether that scout works for them. Legitimate scouts always direct you to official application channels, and real agencies will gladly confirm or deny an impersonator.
