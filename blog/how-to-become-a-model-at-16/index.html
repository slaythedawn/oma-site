How to Become a Model at 16: The Insider Guide for Teens and Parents

By Online Model Academy · The people who book models · August 2026 · 9 min read

If you are 16 and dreaming about modeling, here is something most articles will not tell you: you are exactly on time.

Sixteen and seventeen are the classic scouting ages in fashion. Walk through the new faces division of almost any major agency and you will find teens who were signed at this exact stage, often to development contracts designed to let them finish school while the agency prepares them for the industry.

But because the system is built around teenagers, so are the scams. For every legitimate agent quietly scouting, there are fake "scouts" sliding into DMs and pay-to-play schools promising guaranteed contracts.

This guide walks through how to become a teen model the right way, written for both of you: the teen who wants this, and the parent who wants to protect them. At Online Model Academy, our instructors are working casting directors and agents, so everything below reflects how signings really happen, not how Instagram says they happen.

One rule before anything else: at 16, every step of this process should involve a parent or guardian. That is how legitimate agencies operate, and any professional who tries to cut your parents out of the conversation has just told you everything you need to know about them.

Why 16 to 17 Is the Classic Scouting Age for Fashion

Agencies are not primarily looking for finished, polished 21-year-olds. Fashion agencies scout for potential, and potential shows up early.

Here is the logic from the agency side. Developing a model takes time: learning to move for camera, building a portfolio, doing test shoots, growing into the work. Agencies want that to happen before a model starts working the bigger jobs, not during. Signing at 16 or 17 gives everyone a runway of one to two years, so that by 18, when a model can travel freely and work adult hours, they are ready.

There are practical reasons too:

  • Editorial and high fashion clients skew young. Campaigns and runway castings frequently feature models who started in their mid-to-late teens.
  • Development contracts exist specifically for this age. The industry has a formal structure for signing minors, explained below.
  • Scouts actively look at this age group at events, open calls, and yes, online, which is exactly why teens need to know how to tell real scouts from fake ones.

If you are younger than 16, the industry works differently; our guide on how to become a child model covers that stage. And if you are past your teens, do not panic: commercial and lifestyle modeling welcome a wide range of ages, which we cover in our broader guide on how to become a model.

What a Development Contract Actually Is

This is the single most misunderstood part of teen modeling, so let's slow down.

A development contract (sometimes called a new faces agreement) is how legitimate agencies sign teenagers. The agency is saying: we believe in your potential, and we will invest time developing you before you work full-scale. Under a typical arrangement:

  • The agency arranges test shoots to build your book, coaches you on walking and posing, and introduces you to the industry gradually.
  • Work is limited and scheduled around school. A developing 16-year-old is not expected to book constantly.
  • The agency earns commission only when you book paid work, a percentage of your earnings, commonly around 20 percent. Never fees from you.
  • The contract is signed or co-signed by a parent or guardian, because minors cannot legally sign these agreements alone.

Now the part every teen and parent needs to internalize: agencies sign development contracts based on potential, exactly as you are right now. A real agent looking at a 16-year-old is imagining who you will be at 18, not grading who you are today. Nobody should change their body to fit an application. If an agency's answer is "come back when you have changed your body," thank them and apply elsewhere. Requirements vary widely between fashion, commercial, and specialty boards, and the market is far broader than one agency's taste.

A healthy relationship with food, exercise, and your own reflection will carry you further than any measurement. Agents can develop a walk, a portfolio, and camera skills. They cannot develop a teenager out of a harmful relationship with their body, and they never want to be the cause of one.

How Applications Work When You Are a Minor

Here is the step-by-step, and notice who is involved at every stage: a parent.

1. Research agencies together. Build a family list of legitimate agencies in your region. Look for verifiable client work, real model boards on their websites, a physical business presence, and a track record working models can vouch for.

2. Apply through official channels only. Every legitimate agency has an application page on its website, and many hold scheduled open calls. Those are the only two front doors. Applications for minors should be submitted by, or with, a parent, and most agency forms ask for parent contact details for applicants under 18. If a form does not ask, that is a yellow flag in itself.

3. Send simple digitals, not professional photos. You do not need a portfolio to apply, and any "agency" that requires you to buy a photo package before considering you is selling photos, not representing models.

4. Parents attend every meeting. Any call, video meeting, or in-person interview includes your parent. Legitimate agents expect and prefer this. An "agent" who wants to meet a 16-year-old alone is not an agent.

5. Review any contract with adult eyes. Parents should read every clause and ask about commission structure, expenses, contract length, and how either side can exit. A legitimate agency answers patiently, and no real agency is offended when a family has a lawyer review the agreement.

Balancing School and Modeling (Yes, You Keep Going to School)

Every credible agency expects a 16-year-old to stay in school, full stop. Development contracts are structured around this:

  • Test shoots and castings are typically scheduled for after school, weekends, and school breaks.
  • Bigger opportunities, like market trips or fashion week appearances, are usually planned for summer and holidays.
  • Child labor laws in the US restrict working hours for minors, and reputable clients build shoots around those rules, including required breaks and, in some states, trust accounts (California's Coogan Act is the best-known example) that protect a portion of a minor's earnings.

Parents, treat this as a vetting tool. "We will work around your junior year" is the sound of a professional. "You need to be available whenever we call" is the sound of a problem.

Teens, keeping your grades up is not just a parental demand. Modeling careers are unpredictable, and the models who navigate the industry best are the ones with options and a life outside of castings.

How to Shoot Digitals Safely at Home

Digitals (also called polaroids) are the simple, unedited photos agencies use to evaluate applicants. All you need is a phone, a plain wall, and a parent.

The setup:

  • Natural daylight, facing a window, against a plain wall
  • Simple, fitted clothing: jeans and a plain tank or tee is the classic
  • Minimal or no makeup, hair down and natural, then pulled back for one shot
  • No filters, no editing, no retouching, ever

The shots agencies expect:

  1. Full-length, standing straight, facing camera
  2. Full-length profile
  3. Waist-up, relaxed
  4. Close-up face, neutral expression
  5. Close-up face, natural smile

The safety rules, non-negotiable at 16:

  • A parent takes the photos or is in the room. Always.
  • Digitals go to official agency application portals only. Never to a personal email, a DM, or "a scout who wants to see more."
  • Nothing beyond standard digitals. Any request for revealing or "private" photos, from anyone, is your cue to block, screenshot, and report. A real agency will never ask a minor for anything a parent would not comfortably watch being taken.

The whole shoot takes twenty minutes on a Saturday morning, and it is exactly what new faces directors want.

Red Flags That Target Teens on Social Media

Scammers go where teenagers are, which means the modern modeling scam usually starts in an Instagram or TikTok DM. Here is the pattern-recognition checklist:

  • "You've been scouted!" DMs from unverifiable accounts. Real scouts exist online, but they immediately direct you to the agency's official website, expect your parents to be involved, and check out when your parent calls the agency's listed number. Fake scouts resist verification.
  • Urgency and secrecy. "This offer expires tonight" and "don't tell your parents yet" are grooming tactics, not business practices. Legitimate opportunities survive a parent phone call.
  • Upfront fees. Real agencies earn commission on work you book. Anyone charging registration fees, "casting fees," or mandatory photo packages before representation is running a sales operation.
  • Requests to move the conversation to WhatsApp, Snapchat, or another private channel, or requests for photos beyond standard digitals. Block and report.
  • Flattery that escalates into pressure. Scammers compliment first and pressure second. Professionals are warm but boring: procedural, parent-inclusive, and in no particular hurry.

Teens: make a family rule that every modeling-related message gets shown to a parent before you reply. Parents: keep the tone curious rather than punitive, so your teen actually brings you the messages.

Your First Steps This Month

A realistic starting sequence for a 16-year-old and their parents:

  1. Have the family conversation: goals, boundaries, and the ground rule that everything runs through a parent.
  2. Learn the industry before you enter it: how agencies work, what castings look like, what contracts mean.
  3. Shoot your digitals at home, together.
  4. Apply to your researched agency list through official channels, and treat rejections as routine. Even successful models were passed on by multiple agencies first.
  5. Keep training the fundamentals, walking, posing, on-camera confidence, so you are ready when the callback comes.

FAQ: Becoming a Model at 16

Can you really become a model at 16? Yes. Sixteen and seventeen are the classic scouting ages for fashion modeling, and agencies sign minors through development contracts co-signed by a parent or guardian.

Do I need professional photos to apply to an agency at 16? No. Agencies ask for simple, unedited digitals taken on a phone against a plain wall, shot by a parent and submitted only through an agency's official application page or open call.

Do modeling agencies charge fees to sign a teen? No. Legitimate agencies earn a commission, commonly around 20 percent, on work you actually book. Registration fees, casting fees, and mandatory photo packages are hallmarks of scams targeting teens.

What is a development contract? It is the standard agreement agencies use to sign teenagers based on potential. The agency invests in test shoots, coaching, and gradual industry introductions while the model finishes school, and a parent or guardian signs alongside the minor.

Do I need to change my body to apply? No. Agencies sign teens based on potential as they are, and requirements vary widely across agencies and divisions. If one agency passes, apply to others. No application is worth compromising your health.

Can I balance modeling with school at 16? Yes, and reputable agencies insist on it. Development work is scheduled around school hours and breaks, and US child labor laws add further protections for working minors.
