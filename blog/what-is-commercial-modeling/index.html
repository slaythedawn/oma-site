Types of work

What Is Commercial Modeling? The Job, the Pay and How It Actually Works

By Online Model Academy · The people who book models · August 2026 · 10 min read

Ask most people to picture a model and they picture fashion: the runway, the towering height, the look agencies reject nine applicants out of ten for. That is a real category, and it is also a small one. Most working models, most days, are not doing that. They are doing commercial work: the catalogue shot, the lifestyle ad, the face on a shampoo bottle or a banking app's homepage. It employs more models, books more often, and is where most people who make a real income from modeling actually earn it.

Here is what the job is, from the people who cast it.

What Commercial Modeling Actually Is

Commercial modeling is modeling used to sell something other than the clothes on your back. A client, a brand, a retailer, an agency, needs a photograph or video of a person to sell a product, a service or an idea, and books a model to be that person. The distinction that matters is intent: fashion modeling exists to present the garment, commercial modeling exists to sell whatever the client is selling, and the model's job is to make a stranger want it, trust it, or picture themselves using it.

That single difference in intent changes almost everything else about the category: who gets booked, what a casting brief looks like, and how the work is paid.

How Commercial Differs From Fashion Modeling

Three things separate the two, and understanding them explains most of the confusion beginners have about which lane they actually fit.

What is being sold

A fashion casting is written around a garment: a designer needs a specific silhouette to carry a specific collection, so the brief is built from sample sizing outward. A commercial casting is written around a customer: a brand needs someone who looks like the person who buys their product, so the brief is built from the target buyer outward. A retirement fund ad, a college textbook site and a sneaker drop are selling to three completely different people, and each will cast a model who resembles that person, not a fashion-industry ideal.

Who qualifies

Fashion runway works within a narrow height and proportion range because sample garments are cut to one set of measurements. Commercial has no comparable universal rule, because there is no universal product. A casting for a grandparent in a pharmacy ad, a casting for a teenager in a phone case ad, and a casting for a construction worker in a tool brand's campaign all book completely different bodies, ages and looks, and all three are commercial work. This is the single biggest reason commercial employs so many more working models than fashion: the range of what "books" is genuinely wide, rather than narrow with rare exceptions.

How the work is judged

A fashion casting director is judging whether you disappear into the clothes and carry a specific walk or silhouette. A commercial casting director is judging something closer to relatability on camera: can you look natural rather than posed, can you convey a specific, ordinary emotion, warmth, mild surprise, quiet competence, on command and repeatably, and do you read as a real person a stranger would trust. It is a different skill from a runway walk, and it is arguably harder to fake, because the entire brief is "look like you are not performing."

What Commercial Work Actually Looks Like

"Commercial" covers more ground than most beginners expect. In practice, the bookings a casting director actually runs fall into a handful of recurring types:

  • Catalogue and e-commerce. The product shots on a retailer's own site: on-model apparel, accessories, homeware. High volume, steady demand, and usually the easiest entry point for a new face.
  • Lifestyle. A person using a product in a natural-looking setting, a kitchen, a gym, a car interior, built for a brand's website, social feed or paid ads.
  • Advertising campaigns. Print, digital and broadcast ads for a specific brand push, usually the highest-paying tier of commercial work because of the usage attached to it, covered below.
  • Corporate and tech. Stock-style imagery for software companies, banks, healthcare providers and B2B brands. Unglamorous, consistent, and often underrated as a category by new models chasing more visible work.
  • Food, hospitality and lifestyle-adjacent. Diners, servers, chefs and guests for restaurant, hotel and food-brand campaigns.
  • Hand and parts work. Close-up shots of hands, feet or other features for product photography. A real, specific niche with its own booking standards, covered in more depth in our guide to how to become a hand model.

A single working commercial model might book two or three of these categories in a single year, which is part of why the income tends to be steadier than fashion, even if any one job pays less than a headline runway booking.

Who Actually Gets Booked

The honest answer is: whoever the brief calls for, which is a far wider group than fashion ever considers. There is no universal height, age or size requirement across commercial work, because the category itself has no single client. What consistently matters, across almost every commercial brief, is different from what fashion asks for:

  • A natural, repeatable expression. The specific skill of looking relaxed and unposed on command, take after take, is trainable and it is the single most valuable thing a commercial model brings to a shoot.
  • Range. A model who can read as a parent, a professional, a student or a retiree with a change of styling books more often than one with a single strong look, because it makes them useful across more briefs.
  • Reliability on set. Commercial shoot days are often tightly scheduled around a client's production budget. A model who takes direction quickly and delivers consistent takes without needing dozens of retries is genuinely worth more to a producer than a slightly better-looking model who does not.

This is also why commercial is, by a wide margin, the most accessible entry point into the industry, and it is the category our guide for getting started with no experience points most beginners toward first.

How Commercial Pay Actually Works

The day rate on a commercial booking is only half the number that matters. The other half is usage: what the client is allowed to do with the images afterward, and for how long.

A local retailer licensing a single photo for one product page pays a modest fee. A national brand licensing the same kind of photo for a year of paid social advertising, billboards and TV pays a multiple of that, sometimes a large one, because they are buying the right to put your face in front of millions of people repeatedly rather than once. This is the single most common thing new commercial models get wrong when they read a contract: the day rate looks small next to a runway fee, and the usage line is where the real money, and the real risk of underpricing yourself, actually sits.

We cover the full pay structure, day rates, usage, agency commission and realistic ranges by category, in how much do models actually make. The short version for commercial specifically: it rarely produces a single spectacular payday the way a fashion week show occasionally can, but a model who books consistently across catalogue, lifestyle and advertising work tends to build a steadier, more reliable income over a full year than most fashion-only careers manage.

How to Actually Get Into Commercial Modeling

  1. Shoot simple, natural digitals. Commercial casting directors are looking for how you read unposed, not how well you can pose. Bright, simple, undramatic photos that show a real range of natural expressions outperform a heavily styled portfolio every time. Our guide to building a modeling portfolio covers exactly what to shoot.
  2. Submit to agencies with a commercial or lifestyle board. Not every agency runs one, and some specialise in it entirely. Ask directly, and read our guide to choosing a modeling agency before you sign with anyone.
  3. Expect to start small. Local retailers, regional campaigns and smaller e-commerce clients before a national brand. This is the normal shape of a commercial career building, not a sign of a weak start.
  4. Practise looking unposed on command. It is a genuine, trainable skill, not a personality trait you either have or do not. Casting directors can tell within seconds whether an expression is real or performed.
  5. Read the usage line before you sign anything. A commercial contract's value lives in what the client can do with the photos and for how long, not just in the day rate on the call sheet.

The Short Version

Commercial modeling is the category that sells everything except the clothes themselves, and it is where most working models spend most of their career, whether or not they also do fashion or runway work. It books on relatability, range and reliability rather than a narrow set of proportions, it pays through a combination of day rate and usage rather than a single fee, and it is, by a wide margin, the easiest door into the industry for a beginner to walk through first.
