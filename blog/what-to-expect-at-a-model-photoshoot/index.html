Model Photoshoot: What Really Happens on Set and How to Prepare

By Online Model Academy · The people who book models · October 2026 · 9 min read

The first photoshoot is usually the point where modeling stops being an idea and becomes a day with a call time. It also tends to be nothing like people expect. There is less glamour and more waiting than the photos suggest, and the people who do well are rarely the ones with the most dramatic poses. They are the ones who arrive prepared, stay easy to direct and look after themselves.

Here is how a model photoshoot actually runs, from the people who book models, and how to prepare so the day goes in your favor.

Not All Photoshoots Are the Same

"Photoshoot" covers several very different jobs, and what is expected of you changes with each one.

  • Test shoots. You and a photographer, and sometimes a stylist and makeup artist, create images for everyone's portfolios. Usually unpaid, often called TFP. The point is to build your book, so the pictures matter more than the pay.
  • Commercial shoots. Paid work for a brand, such as product, lifestyle or catalog imagery. There is a client on set, a brief to follow and usually a tight schedule.
  • Editorial shoots. Images for a publication or a creative project, with more room for concept and mood. Pay varies widely and is sometimes minimal.
  • Digitals. Plain, unretouched photos for an agency, with no styling and no concept. Our guide to getting signed by an agency covers what to send.

Ask which of these you are walking into before you say yes. The answer decides what you wear, what you bring and what you should expect to be paid.

Before the Day: What to Confirm

A good booking comes with a clear brief. If yours does not, ask for these in writing:

  1. Date, call time, address and a phone number for someone who will pick up on the day.
  2. The concept, the number of looks and who is providing the wardrobe.
  3. Whether the shoot is paid or a trade, and what you will receive afterward, including how many images and when.
  4. Who will be on set, including any stylist, makeup artist or client.
  5. How the images may be used. If a release will be signed, ask to read it ahead of time.

A vague answer to any of these is information. Clear, professional people answer them without fuss. If someone avoids putting basics in writing, treat that as a reason to slow down.

How to Prepare

Your body and skin

Sleep and water do more than any trick. Skip new skincare, tanning and treatments in the days before, since reactions and marks show on camera. Avoid tight waistbands, bra straps and socks for a few hours before, because the lines can stay on skin and appear in pictures.

Your kit

Bring clean, neutral basics in a few colors, a robe or cover-up, a brush, any products your hair and skin rely on, water and something to eat. Pack the brief, a charger and any items you were asked to bring. If you are providing your own wardrobe, bring more options than you think you need and keep them pressed.

Your practice

Walk in already knowing a handful of poses and expressions. You do not need to plan the whole shoot, but it helps to be comfortable with a few reliable angles. Our guides to how to pose like a model and how to be photogenic cover the fundamentals.

What the Day Actually Looks Like

Most shoots follow the same pattern, even when the budget does not.

  1. Arrival and check-in. Introduce yourself, find out who is who and look at the plan. Arrive a little early. Being late is the quickest way to start a day badly.
  2. Hair and makeup. This can take an hour or more. Sit still, speak up politely if something is uncomfortable and do not alter it yourself afterward without checking.
  3. Wardrobe. Look by look, with a stylist or assistant helping. Keep garments clean and handle them carefully, particularly if they will be returned.
  4. Shooting. One setup at a time. The photographer gives direction, you adjust, and frames get taken in quick bursts. Expect small, specific notes such as "chin down," "weight back" or "relax your hands."
  5. Resets. Between setups there is waiting while lights move and looks change. This is where energy drains, so stay warm, hydrated and in a good mood.
  6. Wrap. Thank everyone, confirm what happens next and ask when you can expect images or payment.

The biggest surprise for new models is the pace. Shooting is quick and repetitive, and most frames are variations on a small number of moves. The skill is staying fresh through that repetition.

How to Be Easy to Work With

Photographers and bookers remember the people who made the day smoother. That comes down to a few habits.

  • Take direction cleanly. Make the change you were asked for, then hold it. Do not explain why a pose feels awkward unless something is actually painful.
  • Repeat what works. If the photographer reacts well to a frame, say "same again" and give it to them.
  • Keep your phone away. Checking it between setups breaks the energy and signals you would rather be elsewhere.
  • Be kind to the whole crew. Assistants, stylists and makeup artists talk, and they are often the ones recommending models to the next client.

Staying Safe

Most photographers are professionals running a normal working day. A small number are not, and new models are the people they target, so a few simple habits are worth building now.

  • Check a photographer's published work, references and how long they have been operating before agreeing to a one-on-one shoot.
  • Tell someone you trust where you are, who you are with and when you expect to finish. Bring a friend or chaperone for a first test shoot if you can.
  • Agree on the concept and wardrobe beforehand, including what you will not do. A professional accepts those limits without negotiation.
  • You can stop at any time. Pressure to wear less, do more or "just trust me" is a reason to leave, not a reason to push through.
  • Be wary of any shoot that is required before an agency will consider you. Reputable agencies do not make representation depend on paying for photography. Our guide to choosing a modeling agency explains the pattern.

Paperwork, Pay and What Happens After

Read anything you are asked to sign, especially a model release. Check where and for how long the images can be used, whether the use is limited to a specific purpose and whether there are any extra uses you did not expect. Paid jobs should come with a rate and payment terms in writing. Our guide to how to get paid as a model covers invoices and timelines.

After the shoot, ask when you will receive images and what you are allowed to do with them. For test shoots, you should be able to use selected images in your portfolio. Check whether credit lines are expected and whether retouching is limited.

Making the Most of Your First Shoot

Treat the first shoot as practice you happen to be photographed doing. Notice what you found easy, which directions threw you and which frames felt natural. Ask the photographer which images worked best and why. That feedback is worth more than most of the pictures.
