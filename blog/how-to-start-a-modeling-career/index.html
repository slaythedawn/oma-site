How to Start a Modeling Career: The Steps in Order

By Online Model Academy · The people who book models · August 2026 · 12 min read

Most guides to starting a modeling career are a list of encouragements. This one is the sequence the people who actually sign and book models would put you through, in the order that matters.

Our instructors at Online Model Academy are working agents, casting directors and runway producers. Here is the route as they describe it.

Step 1: Work Out What You Actually Are

Before a single photo or email, decide which category you fit. This is the step everyone skips and the reason most submissions fail.

  • Fashion and runway — women roughly 5'9" to 6'0", men roughly 6'0" to 6'3". The range is driven by sample sizing, not preference.
  • Commercial — the largest board and where most models earn most of their money. No meaningful height requirement. Clients are casting believable people.
  • Curve and plus — its own board with its own clients. See our guide to breaking in and our curve board guide.
  • Parts — hands, feet, hair, skin. Specialist, well paid hourly.
  • Fit — stable specific measurements, used in garment development. Steady and almost invisible from outside.
  • Classic — over roughly 35, consistently under-supplied.

That is the short version. For the full map of categories, what each pays, and how to tell which one actually fits you, see our guide to the different types of modeling.

Be honest here. Aiming at a fashion board at 5'5" is not ambition, it is a guaranteed no from every agency you contact — while the commercial board that would have taken you never hears from you.

Step 2: Measure Yourself Properly

Height, bust or chest, waist, hips, dress or suit size, shoe size, hair colour, eye colour. Measured, not estimated, in the units of your market.

Agencies remeasure. Arriving shorter than you claimed tells an agent something about your reliability before anyone has looked at your face.

Step 3: Shoot Digitals

Not a photoshoot. Digitals.

  • Four to six photos on a phone, in daylight, against a plain wall.
  • Front, side, back. One smiling, one neutral. Full-length and a face close-up.
  • No makeup, no filters, no retouching. Hair down and natural.
  • Fitted plain clothes. Heels for women if relevant to your board.

Agencies specifically want to see you unhelped. A polished, retouched submission reads as concealment and guarantees a wasted meeting. This costs nothing and takes twenty minutes — see our no-experience guide for the detail.

Step 4: Build a Real Agency Shortlist

Not every agency, and not the nearest one. Agencies that run a board for your category, in a market you can physically reach for castings.

Verify each one: does it earn commission rather than fees, can you trace its clients and campaigns, do the models on its board visibly work, does it have a real office? Our guides to finding a real agency and choosing between agencies cover the full checklist.

If you are in Australia, our Australian agency guide maps the market city by city.

Step 5: Submit Exactly as Asked

Every agency publishes a submission process. Follow it precisely. Send what is requested, in the format requested, to the address requested, and nothing extra.

A surprising share of rejections are administrative rather than aesthetic: professional photos sent when digitals were asked for, missing stats, files that will not open, a five-paragraph covering letter nobody read.

Step 6: Go to Open Calls

The most direct route into a room with a booker, and consistently underused. Expect two to five minutes, a wall photo, a measurement, and "we'll be in touch" — that is the format working, not a rejection. Our open casting call guide covers how to prepare.

Step 7: Understand What Happens After Signing

Signing is the start, not the finish. What typically follows:

  • Testing. Shoots arranged by your agency to build your book. Often unpaid on both sides, and normal.
  • Your comp card. The agency designs it. See our comp card guide.
  • Castings. Frequently, at short notice, mostly unsuccessful. This is the job.
  • The first booking. Usually months in, usually small.

Models who quit almost always quit during this phase, because the gap between being signed and being booked is longer and quieter than anyone expects.

Step 8: Learn How the Money Works

A booking pays a day or session rate plus a usage fee covering where the images run and for how long. Usage is frequently the larger figure and the part new models forget to ask about. Our guide to where modeling work comes from breaks the structure down, and our guide to how models actually get paid covers what happens after the booking, from invoicing through to the money actually landing.

Your genuine costs starting out are casting travel, basic grooming and any training you choose. Representation itself should cost nothing, ever. Our guide to what modeling costs puts real numbers on it.

The Realistic Timeline

  • Week 1 — category decided, measurements taken, digitals shot.
  • Weeks 2–4 — agencies researched, submissions sent, open calls attended.
  • Months 1–3 — responses, meetings, possibly representation. Silence from most.
  • Months 3–9 — testing, book building, castings, first bookings.
  • Year 1+ — a working pattern, if the category fit was right.

Anyone promising to compress this is selling something.

What Separates the Models Who Last

Not looks — everyone at a casting is castable. It is the craft: how you walk, how you take direction, how you handle a long day, how you behave when a client changes the brief at the last minute. Those things get you rebooked, and rebooking is the entire career.
