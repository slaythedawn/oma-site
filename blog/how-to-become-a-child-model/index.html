For parents

How to Become a Child Model: A Parent's Honest Guide

By Online Model Academy · The people who book models · August 2026 · 10 min read

People stop you in the grocery store to say your kid should be in commercials, and somewhere in the back of your mind you are wondering: is this actually a thing families do, and how would we even start?

Here is the honest answer, from the side of the table that books kids for a living. Child modeling is a real industry with real paychecks. It is also one of the most scam-heavy corners of entertainment, precisely because it runs on parental hope. The good news: the legitimate path is simple, mostly free to enter, and easy to tell apart from the fake one once you know what to look for. Safety first, always. Bookings second.

How Child Modeling Actually Works

Start by letting go of the fashion-show image in your head. The overwhelming majority of child modeling work is commercial: catalog and e-commerce shoots for clothing brands, advertising for toys and family products, packaging photography, and lifestyle imagery for everything from theme parks to insurance companies. Brands need pictures of real-looking kids using their products, and someone has to be those kids.

The people who book them form a short chain. A brand or its ad agency hires a casting director or goes straight to modeling agencies with a brief: "we need a six-year-old with a big natural laugh who can ride a scooter." Agencies submit kids from their roster, the client picks from photos or holds a casting, and the agency handles the contract, the invoice, and its commission. What that chain means for you as a parent is important: legitimate work flows through agencies and casting professionals, not through Instagram DMs from strangers or "talent scouts" at the mall.

What the Work Looks Like by Age

Babies and toddlers (roughly 0 to 3). Diaper brands, baby gear, food packaging, family advertising. Bookings are unpredictable because the "talent" cannot take direction, and babies age out of size ranges in months. Agencies here care almost entirely about temperament and parental flexibility, since castings can come with a day's notice.

Young kids (roughly 4 to 9). The sweet spot for catalog and e-commerce work. Kidswear brands shoot constantly, and a child who can follow direction and stay cheerful through wardrobe changes is genuinely valuable. Toy campaigns and family commercials live here too.

Tweens (roughly 10 to 12). Catalog work continues, and sample sizes matter more. Fit modeling, where a child tries on garments so a brand can check sizing, is steady, unglamorous work for kids with consistent measurements.

Teens (13 and up). The industry transitions toward the adult fashion market, and by 16 some teens work in mainstream fashion under extra protections, a stage we walk through in our guide to signing with an agency as a teenager. If your child is in this band, our guide on how to become a model covers that path in detail.

What Agencies Actually Look For (It Is Mostly Temperament, and You)

Here is the part that surprises almost every parent: for children, looks matter far less than you think. Brands want kids who look like kids. All ethnicities, all hair types, glasses, freckles, gap teeth. Catalogs are trying to reflect the families who shop from them, so there is no single "model look" for children the way there is in adult high fashion.

What agencies are really screening for in the room:

  • Comfort with strangers. Can your child work with a photographer and stylist they met four minutes ago and stay relaxed? A kid who clings or freezes is not a bad kid, just not ready for this environment.
  • Ability to take direction. "Look over here, now jump, now do it again but slower." A child who can listen and repeat is worth more than a beautiful child who cannot.
  • Genuine expression on demand. The kids who work constantly are the ones whose real laugh shows up when asked. Agencies can spot a rehearsed pose from across the room.
  • Stamina and patience. Shoots involve waiting, wardrobe changes, and repetition. Fun, but tiring.

And then there is the evaluation nobody tells you about: the agency is signing the parent as much as the child. To a booker, a wonderful kid with a difficult parent is an unbookable package. Will this parent answer emails the same day? Show up early with a fed, rested child? Stay calm when a casting is cancelled at 5 p.m.? Stay off set instead of coaching from behind the camera? Organized, punctual, low-drama parents get their kids seen more. That is not a rumor. It is how submission lists get made.

The Application Process for Minors, Step by Step

The legitimate route is refreshingly cheap and boring.

1. Research agencies with genuine kids' divisions. Look for established agencies in or near a real market city (New York, Los Angeles, Chicago, Miami, Atlanta, Dallas, and other regional hubs), with a visible kids' board on their website, verifiable clients, and any licensing your state requires for talent agencies.

2. Submit simple snapshots, not professional photos. This is the number one thing parents get wrong. Agencies want current, natural, well-lit phone photos: one smiling face shot, one full body, plain clothes, no filters, no makeup, no posing. Kids change so fast that professional portfolios are outdated in months. Any agency that requires a photo package before considering your child is a photo sales business, not an agency.

3. Submit online or attend an open call. A parent or legal guardian handles every step; no legitimate professional communicates with your child directly.

4. If invited in, treat the meeting as a two-way interview. Ask how commission works (a percentage of earnings, often 10 to 20 percent for print), what clients they book for, and what they expect from parents. A good agency respects a prepared parent.

5. Read the contract like the legal document it is. You, as guardian, sign on your child's behalf. Look at term length, exclusivity, commission, and especially image rights: who can use your child's photos, where, and for how long. If anything is unclear, have a lawyer familiar with talent contracts review it first.

6. Expect silence, and do not pay to break it. Agencies pass on most submissions, often without replying. Resubmit in six months with new snapshots. A "no" from one agency means nothing about the next.

Child Labor Law Basics, in Plain Language

State laws for working children genuinely vary, so treat this section as orientation, not legal advice. Before your child works a single job, check your own state's labor department rules for minors in entertainment.

Work permits. Many states require a child performer or entertainment work permit before a minor can work a shoot, and some also require the employer to hold a permit to hire minors. Applications typically involve a parent, proof of age, sometimes school sign-off, and renewal.

Coogan accounts. California requires a portion of a child performer's gross earnings, generally 15 percent, to go into a blocked trust account, often called a Coogan account, that the child controls at adulthood. The law exists because an earlier generation of child stars reached 18 and found their money gone. New York and a handful of other states have similar trust requirements, and even where the law is silent, setting aside earnings in your child's name is simply good practice. This is your child's money, not household income.

On-set rules. States commonly limit working hours by age, mandate rest and meal breaks, restrict how late children can be on set, and require schooling arrangements on longer jobs. Infants have the strictest limits.

A parent or guardian on set. In most jurisdictions, and as standard industry practice everywhere, a parent or designated guardian must accompany a minor and stay within sight or sound of the child. Any production that discourages this is telling you everything you need to know.

Reputable productions handle this paperwork routinely. A client annoyed by permits and guardianship rules is a client to walk away from.

Safety and Scam Protection: The Non-Negotiables

This industry's dark side targets parents, because love makes people reachable. Commit these rules to memory.

Never pay to be signed. This is the bright line. Legitimate agencies earn money when your child earns money, through commission. Upfront "registration fees," mandatory photo shoots or classes as a condition of representation, and "casting platform" subscriptions pitched by a scout are all versions of the same scam. The product being sold is your hope.

Be skeptical of mall scouts and DM scouts. Anyone who approaches your family cold and creates urgency ("spots close Friday") is running a sales funnel, not a casting.

Chaperone rules are absolute. Your child is never alone at a casting, fitting, or shoot. You stay within sight or sound. No legitimate professional will ever ask to be alone with a child or communicate with your child directly instead of through you.

Guard photo rights. Usage terms define where images appear and for how long; be cautious about granting perpetual, unlimited rights to anyone. Think hard before building a public social media presence around your child's face, because publicly posted photos of children can be saved and misused by anyone. Private is the safer default.

Trust the pit of your stomach. If a situation feels off, leave. No booking is worth overriding your instincts about your child's safety, and a professional set will never make you choose.

What It Really Costs Your Family: Time and Money

Money first, because it is simple: entering legitimately costs almost nothing. Snapshots are free, agency submission is free, and nothing significant should leave your wallet before your child has representation and work. If someone is charging thousands to "launch" a child's career, revisit the scam section above. For a fuller breakdown of legitimate versus illegitimate expenses, see our guide on how much it costs to become a model.

Time is the real price, and it lands on the parent. Castings are typically weekdays, in business hours, in the nearest market city, often announced a day or two ahead. That can mean leaving work early, driving an hour for a five-minute casting, and doing it repeatedly without a booking, because even successful kids hear far more nos than yeses. Pay varies widely by market, client, and usage, but income is irregular and should never be part of the family budget.

The right frame: this is an interesting paid activity, like a sport with occasional prize money, not a career strategy. Families who thrive treat it lightly. The moment it stops being fun for the child is the moment to stop, and the child gets a permanent, no-questions-asked veto.

The Bottom Line for Parents

Child modeling is legitimate, learnable, and nearly free to try: snapshots, agency submissions, patience, and a firm rule that money flows toward your child, never away. Your job is not stage parenting. It is logistics, paperwork, and being the calm adult who protects the child's boundaries while the professionals work.

FAQ

What age can a child start modeling? There is no minimum. Agencies represent newborns through teens. The practical question is not age but readiness: babies need flexible parents, and older kids need the temperament to work comfortably with strangers and take direction.

Do child models need professional photos to get an agency? No, and any "agency" that requires paid photos before representation is a red flag. Agencies want current phone snapshots: a clear face photo and a full-length photo in simple clothes, no filters or makeup.

How much do child models get paid? It varies widely, from modest e-commerce day rates to substantially more for national campaigns. Agencies take commission from earnings, and in some states a portion must go into a blocked trust for the child. Treat it as an irregular bonus, never expected family money.

Do child models need a work permit? In many states, yes. Requirements differ by state and cover permits, working hours, breaks, schooling, and trust accounts such as California's Coogan accounts. Check your state labor department's rules before any job. Legitimate productions expect this paperwork.

How do I know if a child modeling agency is legitimate? Legitimate agencies never charge fees to join, earn commission only when your child works, have verifiable clients and a real kids' board, and are licensed where required. Scam operations charge upfront fees, sell photo packages or classes as a condition of signing, and use pressure tactics like expiring offers.

Can my child model if we do not live near a big city? Yes, though with fewer opportunities. Regional markets have agencies and local commercial work, and some brands cast remotely from digital submissions. Most shoots still happen in market cities, so distance mainly costs time and gas rather than making modeling impossible.
